Both omissions can be put back into the engine. We add to the rule a logistic error of scale $\sigma$, drawn once per person and simulation and shared by each network and its twin, and in a second variant we also drop the requirement of an adopting contact, so that anyone whose noisy aversion falls below $\Gamma$ adopts with no exposure. In ELSOC, the 15\% of the less averse ($q \le 0.5$) who take part with no exposure corresponds to $\sigma \approx 0.3$ at the estimated $\Gamma$, or to 0.44 (95\% CI 0.32--0.76) when the rule is fitted on the unexposed alone; the $\hat\sigma$ of the full rule, 0.48, absorbs measurement error and bounds it from above. Noise alone barely moves the premium up to $\sigma = 0.25$ (between $-0.9$ and $+0.5$ points, averaged over $\lambda$ from 0.5 to 1.5) and lowers it by 1 to 2 points at $\sigma = 0.5$. Spontaneous adoption costs more, because it lifts both topologies from below: dropping the contact requirement alone takes 1.7 to 2.2 points, and with $\sigma = 0.25$ the premium is 5.3 to 6.7 points, with $\sigma = 0.5$ 3.6 to 4.9, between 40 and 54\% of its deterministic value. It stays positive in all four surveys, at every $\lambda$ and every level of noise (the lowest value is 1.5 points, in ELSOC with $\sigma = 0.5$ and $\lambda = 0.5$, and no interval reaches zero), and the network still reaches half adoption at a gate 0.06 to 0.13 narrower than its twin's. Noise moves the reverse corner rather than removing it: at $\sigma = 0.25$ robust cells reappear at $\lambda$ from 1.0 to 1.4, at low quality ($\Gamma \le 0.2$) with an almost open gate, and in the three surveys where aversion is tied to the attributes that sort ties, permuting it removes nearly all of them; at $\sigma = 0.5$ there are none. The deterministic rule therefore sets an upper bound on the size of the premium, too high by up to a factor of two at the noise the data allow, but the premium does not depend on it.
